THE RULES OF CHESS (FIDE Laws, extended reference)

0. NATURE OF THE GAME
Chess is a two-player game of perfect information played on a chequered board.
Each side commands an army of sixteen pieces, and the two armies are told apart
only by their shade: one army is of the lighter tint, the other of the darker
tint. Throughout this reference we speak of "the light army" and "the dark army",
of "light squares" and "dark squares", never naming an actual colour. The object
is to trap the enemy king so that it cannot escape capture — a state called
checkmate. There is no element of chance; every outcome follows from the moves
the two players choose.

1. THE BOARD
The board is a square of eight ranks and eight files, giving sixty-four squares
in all. The squares alternate between a lighter tint and a darker tint, and the
board is oriented so that each player has a light square at the near-right
corner ("light on right"). The ranks are numbered 1 to 8 counting away from the
light army's side; the files are lettered a to h from the light army's left to
its right. Every square therefore has a unique coordinate such as e4, g7, or a1.
The a1 corner square lies on the darker tint; the h1 corner lies on the lighter
tint. A "diagonal" is a line of squares of a single tint running corner to
corner. The four central squares — d4, d5, e4, e5 — form the centre, the most
valuable real estate on the board.

2. THE PIECES AND THEIR STARTING POSITION
Each side has one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights, and
eight pawns. On the first rank the pieces stand, from the a-file across: rook,
knight, bishop, queen, king, bishop, knight, rook. The queen begins on a square
of her own tint (the light queen on a light square, the dark queen on a dark
square); the king stands beside her. The eight pawns fill the entire second rank
in front of the pieces. The light army occupies ranks 1 and 2; the dark army
occupies ranks 7 and 8. The light army always makes the opening move of the
game, and the dark army replies; thereafter the two sides alternate, one move at
a time, and a player may never decline to move when it is their turn.

3. HOW THE PIECES MOVE
- The king steps one square in any direction — along a rank, a file, or a
  diagonal — provided the destination is not attacked by an enemy piece.
- The queen, the most powerful piece, slides any number of empty squares along a
  rank, a file, or a diagonal.
- A rook slides any number of empty squares along a rank or a file.
- A bishop slides any number of empty squares along a diagonal; because a
  diagonal never changes tint, each bishop is confined forever to squares of a
  single tint. One bishop travels the light squares, its partner the dark.
- A knight leaps in an L-shape: two squares along one line and then one square
  perpendicular. Alone among the pieces it jumps over anything in its path, and
  it always lands on a square of the opposite tint to the one it left.
- A pawn advances one square straight forward toward the far side, or two squares
  forward from its own starting rank, and it captures one square diagonally
  forward. A pawn can never move backward.
A piece captures by moving onto a square occupied by an enemy piece, which is
then removed from the board. The king is never actually captured; instead the
game ends when its capture cannot be prevented.

4. SPECIAL MOVES
- Castling is a single move of the king and one rook. The king moves two squares
  toward the rook, and that rook leaps to the square the king has just crossed.
  It is permitted only if neither the king nor that rook has moved before, if the
  squares between them are empty, and if the king is not in check, does not pass
  through an attacked square, and does not land on one. Castling on the king's
  own wing is "short"; on the queen's wing it is "long".
- En passant ("in passing"): if a pawn advances two squares and thereby lands
  beside an enemy pawn, that enemy pawn may, on the very next move only, capture
  it as though it had advanced a single square.
- Promotion: a pawn that reaches the far rank is at once exchanged for a queen,
  rook, bishop, or knight of its own army — the player chooses, and almost always
  chooses a queen. A player may thus, in principle, come to hold several queens.

5. CHECK, CHECKMATE, AND STALEMATE
A king attacked by an enemy piece is "in check". The player in check must remove
the attack at once, by one of three means: capturing the attacker, blocking the
line of attack with another piece, or moving the king to a safe square. A player
may never make a move that leaves, or places, their own king in check. If a king
is in check and there is no legal move that removes the attack, the position is
checkmate and the game ends immediately; the side delivering mate wins. If the
player to move is not in check but has no legal move at all, the position is
stalemate, and the game is drawn.

6. HOW A GAME IS DECIDED
A game is won by delivering checkmate, or when the opponent resigns, or when the
opponent oversteps the time limit. A game is drawn by stalemate; by mutual
agreement; by threefold repetition of the identical position; by the fifty-move
rule (fifty consecutive moves by each side with no capture and no pawn move); or
when insufficient material remains for either side to force checkmate — for
example a lone king against a lone king. In competition a win scores one point, a
draw half a point each, and a loss zero.

7. NOTATION
Moves are written in algebraic notation. Each piece has a letter — K for king, Q
for queen, R for rook, B for bishop, N for knight — while a pawn move is written
by its destination square alone. Thus Nf3 means a knight moves to f3, and e4
means a pawn moves to e4. A capture is marked with an "x" (Bxe5), check with "+",
checkmate with "#". Castling short is written O-O and castling long O-O-O. When
two like pieces could reach the same square, the file or rank of origin is added
to remove doubt.

8. PHASES OF A GAME
Play divides loosely into three phases. In the OPENING the two sides develop
their pieces off the back rank, fight for the centre, and bring the king to
safety by castling; sound principles are to occupy the centre, to move each piece
but once until development is complete, and to castle early. In the MIDDLEGAME
the armies clash: players form plans, create threats, combine pieces into
attacks, and weigh sacrifices of material for initiative. In the ENDGAME, with
few pieces left, the king turns from a liability into a fighting piece, and the
promotion of a pawn to a queen often decides the outcome.

9. RELATIVE VALUE OF THE PIECES
As a rough guide, a pawn is worth one point, a knight and a bishop about three
each, a rook five, and a queen nine; the king is beyond price, since losing it
loses the game. These values are only a guide: an active knight may outweigh a
passive rook, and a well-timed sacrifice may be worth far more than the material
it gives up. Two bishops working together are reckoned slightly stronger than a
bishop and a knight, because the pair between them can cover squares of either
tint.

10. CONDUCT AND THE CLOCK
Serious games are played with a chess clock that gives each side a fixed budget
of time; a player whose time runs out loses, unless the opponent has no way to
mate, in which case the game is drawn. Under the touch-move law a player who
deliberately touches one of their own pieces must move it if it has a legal move,
and a player who touches an enemy piece must capture it if that is legal; to
adjust a piece without moving it a player first says "I adjust". Moves are
completed with the same hand that presses the clock. Players must behave so as
not to distract or annoy the opponent, and the arbiter oversees fair play.
