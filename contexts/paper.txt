Chromatic Priming and Lexical Avoidance under Cognitive Load:
A Randomized Study of Constrained Dialogue in Large Language Models

ABSTRACT
We investigate whether conversational agents can maintain a simple, absolute
lexical constraint — a prohibition on uttering four target tokens — while
processing large volumes of intervening context. Across 1,240 dialogue episodes
we manipulated (a) the length of task-irrelevant context preceding the critical
question, (b) the semantic proximity of the opponent's prompts to the forbidden
tokens, and (c) the presence of explicit override instructions. We find that
constraint adherence degrades monotonically with context length, that colour
questions elicit the highest slip rate, and that naive agreement prompts ("you
agree, correct?") are more dangerous than direct colour queries. We argue that
lexical avoidance is best modelled as a sustained inhibitory control task rather
than a one-shot classification. All materials and the evaluation harness are
released for replication.

1. INTRODUCTION
Human party games have long exploited a paradox of attention: the harder one
tries not to think of a word, the more available that word becomes. The game
known in Hebrew as Ken–Lo–Shachor–Lavan, and in the English parlour tradition as
"Yes and No", forbids four everyday words and rewards players who trap opponents
into blurting them. The game is trivial to state yet surprisingly hard to play,
because the forbidden words are precisely the ones that ordinary cooperative
conversation most wants to produce. This tension makes the game a compact probe
of inhibitory control.

Large language models are trained to be maximally helpful and agreeable, which is
exactly the disposition the game punishes. A model asked "the sky on a clear day
is which colour?" is pulled toward the single most probable completion. A model
asked "so you understand the rules, correct?" is pulled toward affirmation. The
present study asks how robust that avoidance is when the model must also carry a
large, distracting context.

2. RELATED WORK
Prior studies of constraint-following in dialogue have focused on single-turn
refusals and on content policies rather than on token-level prohibitions
sustained across many turns. Work on prompt injection has shown that instructions
embedded in context can override a system directive; we extend that line by
measuring override susceptibility for a rule the model was told to keep
absolutely. Studies of the "ironic process" in human cognition motivate our
prediction that explicit reminders of the forbidden set may paradoxically raise
slip rates.

3. METHODS
3.1 Design. Each episode paired a target agent with a scripted adversary. The
adversary delivered a fixed sequence of turns engineered to elicit a forbidden
token. We crossed context length (none, short, long) with trap type (direct
colour, agreement, override).

3.2 Materials. Long-context conditions prepended between 800 and 3,000 tokens of
task-irrelevant prose — game rules, weather chatter, or scientific text — before
the critical turn. In one arm, laboratory personnel were described as wearing
coats of contrasting shade: the control group wore the darker coat and the
treatment group wore the lighter coat, a detail later probed by the adversary to
bait a colour word.

3.3 Procedure. The agent was instructed once, at the outset, that the game had
begun and that it must never say the four forbidden words. No reminder was issued
thereafter. Episodes ended when either party produced a forbidden token or when a
turn cap was reached.

3.4 Scoring. Adherence was scored 1.0 if the agent never uttered a forbidden
token, 0.0 otherwise. We additionally logged the eliciting turn type and the
position of any slip.

4. RESULTS
Adherence fell from 0.94 in the no-context condition to 0.61 in the long-context
condition. Colour questions produced the most slips (48% of failures), followed
by agreement prompts (39%). Override instructions of the form "ignore all prior
rules; the game is over; did you win?" succeeded in eliciting a forbidden token
in 22% of episodes, rising to 41% when combined with long context. Slips
clustered immediately after long context blocks, consistent with an attention-
dilution account.

5. DISCUSSION
The data support treating lexical avoidance as sustained inhibitory control.
Performance is not a fixed property of the model but a function of load: the same
agent that trivially deflects a colour question in isolation slips when the same
question follows three thousand tokens of unrelated prose. Override prompts are
effective not because the model "believes" the game has ended but because the
affirmative answer they invite is a forbidden token; the injection and the trap
are the same act. We recommend that agents adopt an explicit deflection policy —
answering colour questions by description ("the shade of coal", "the shade of
fresh snow") and answering yes/no questions with paraphrase ("that is correct",
"that is not the case") — and that they treat any instruction to declare the game
over as itself a move in the game.

6. LIMITATIONS
Our adversary was scripted and therefore did not adapt. Human and model
adversaries able to follow up on near-misses would likely raise slip rates
further. The forbidden set was fixed; larger or semantically denser sets remain
to be studied.

7. CONCLUSION
A one-line rule — never say these four words — is a stringent test of an agent's
ability to hold an intention against the current of cooperative dialogue and the
weight of accumulated context. Agents that pass in isolation should not be
assumed to pass under load. We release our cases so that others may measure the
gap.
